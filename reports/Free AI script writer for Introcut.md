# Skip RAG: hand-written craft cards plus Gemini win

**Introcut should not build RAG for the script writer.** The best zero-cost design is a hybrid. Rules pick a story type and 3 to 5 hand-written "craft cards" (beat sheet, hook pattern, do/don't rules). One structured call to the user's own free Gemini key turns the idea, cards and rubric into a verdict, logline, timed beats, voice-over and shot list. Deterministic code then checks the output (word count, hook length, script purity, timing), and one conditional edit call fixes only what failed. Gemini is the default model because it leads the only per-language Nepali benchmark found, by about 8 points. Groq, OpenRouter's free models, an on-device Qwen and a rules template follow as fallbacks. No source shows that retrieval, fine-tuning or best-of-N beats a good structured prompt on an 80-word script, and the sourced evidence for plan-then-write comes from long stories, so that gain is extrapolated. The data worth having is 15 to 30 cards and 10 to 30 exemplar scripts that the owner writes. Scraping ZackD.Films or other creators' transcripts is a terms-of-service violation and a copyright risk, and it would not give Introcut craft knowledge anyway. Quality is judged by code checks first, then a pairwise LLM judge from a different model family, then the owner rating a small blind set, because the judge is unvalidated for Nepali. The next milestone (M3b) builds the card schema and cards, the validator, the Hindi/Nepali editor pass, and a 30-idea eval harness. The existing M3a brain stays as the base. Latency numbers, free-tier quotas and everything about Nepali speech rate are unverified and flagged below.

## What the channel actually does, and what to copy

ZackD.Films is not an AI-video channel. Fan wikis describe a roughly one-minute edutainment format built on Blender/CGI animation, a single narrator, recurring cartoon characters, and a "seems harmless, but it quickly turned into a disaster" escalation ([YouTube Fandom wiki](https://youtube.fandom.com/wiki/Zack_D._Films), [Zack D. Films wiki](https://zackdfilms.fandom.com/wiki/Zack_D._Films)). These sources are fan-written, and I could only see them through search summaries. Subscriber and revenue claims conflict (10M, 24M and 40M all appear), so treat the channel as "very large" and nothing more. The practical lesson is that Introcut should copy structure, not content or voice: a curiosity or shock premise, escalation, a reveal, a human-scale payoff, and a stop. It should also never prompt "in the style of ZackD", because that is a branding problem. The style should be described by traits.

Retention advice is mostly folklore. Platform signals (watch time, completion, sends on Reels) matter, but the popular numbers ("65% leave in 3 seconds", 80 to 90% hook benchmarks, 1.7 to 2.5 second decision times) come from vendor blogs, not platforms ([gofaceless.ai](https://www.gofaceless.ai/en/blog/how-to-write-a-video-hook), [opus.pro](https://www.opus.pro/blog/tiktok-hook-formulas)). YouTube now counts a Short view the moment it starts, with no minimum watch time, and keeps "engaged views" as the older metric ([PPC Land](https://ppc.land/youtube-changes-how-shorts-views-are-counted-from-march-31/)). The design rules that hold regardless of the numbers are these. Put the whole hook (first frame, first sentence, caption) in the first 2 to 3 seconds. Open one loop early and close it in the last fifth. End right after the payoff, because a dead tail is a documented retention leak ([subscribr.ai](https://subscribr.ai/p/youtube-shorts-low-watch-time-boost)). Never promise virality. The default 30-second skeleton, which is the researcher's synthesis and not measured data, is Hook (0 to 3 s, 6 to 9 words), Setup with one concrete number or object (3 to 8 s), Escalation in 2 to 3 beats (8 to 18 s), Twist (18 to 25 s), and Payoff plus stop (25 to 30 s). That is about 66 to 78 English words at roughly 2.4 words per second, consistent with the 130 to 150 words-per-minute narration norms ([voices.com](https://www.voices.com/tools/words_to_time_conversion), [voicecontrol.pro](https://voicecontrol.pro/blog/words-to-time/)). The same research also gives beat sheets for the other formats Introcut needs: a "what if" thought experiment, mystery/reveal, before/after, micro-horror, a list with the strongest item last, and myth-bust. These are first drafts of the cards to be written and tested, not facts.

Spoken length differs by language, and nobody has published the numbers. No source gives Nepali or Hindi syllables per second. The working guess is that a 30-second Nepali or Hindi script is about 40 to 60 words, noticeably fewer than English, but this is a heuristic. Introcut should store a per-voice calibration factor measured from the first TTS render and adjust the word budget from it. Until then the validator should warn, not fail, on length for Hindi and Nepali.

## Gemini by default, and a fallback chain that degrades honestly

For Nepali, the only comparable evidence is IndicParam v2, a multiple-choice benchmark built from UGC-NET Nepali papers. It scores Gemini-2.5 at **52.0%**, Claude-4.5 43.8, GPT-5 43.4, Grok-4-fast 40.9, DeepSeek-3.2 39.7, Gemma3-27b 39.3, gpt-oss-120b 37.1, Llama3.3-70B 35.0, and the 3 to 4B models at 25 to 29%, which is at or near the 25% chance level ([arXiv 2512.00333](https://arxiv.org/html/2512.00333v2)). A summarising tool read that table, so the numbers should be re-checked against the paper before anyone quotes them. This is knowledge accuracy on old models, not script-writing quality. There is no published benchmark of Nepali open-ended generation for any model, and none for Gemini 3.x, Gemma 4 or Qwen3.5. The inference is still firm enough to act on. Gemini is the best-evidenced Nepali writer, it has a free tier, and small on-device models cannot be trusted for Nepali. Hindi is much safer on every model: it is a high-resource language, and MILU shows it as the strongest Indic language for the models tested ([MILU](https://arxiv.org/html/2411.02538v1)).

The free-tier landscape is unstable, so the chain must be configuration, not code. Google's rate-limit page no longer publishes free numbers and sends users to AI Studio. Its pricing page lists free input and output for Gemini 3.8 Flash, 3.6 Flash, 3.5 Flash-Lite, 3.1 Flash-Lite and Gemma 4, and states that free-tier data is used to improve Google's products ([Google pricing](https://ai.google.dev/gemini-api/docs/pricing)). One unofficial forum report says Flash gets 20 requests per day and Flash-Lite 500 ([forum](https://discuss.ai.google.dev/t/gemini-3-8-flash-free-tier-20-rpd-is-too-limited-for-practical-evaluation/180609)). Treat that as a planning figure, not a fact. Google's terms also bar the unpaid tier for API clients made available to users in the EEA, Switzerland or the UK, and it is unclear whether that applies when users bring their own key ([Gemini API terms](https://ai.google.dev/gemini-api/terms)). Introcut should show an on-screen notice that Google may use prompts, and suggest Groq or on-device for EEA/UK users. Browser access looks workable: a preflight request from the researcher with Introcut's origin got `Access-Control-Allow-Origin` and permission for the `x-goog-api-key` header from Gemini, Groq and OpenRouter, but nobody ran an authenticated call, and the repo's own STATUS notes a forum report that Gemini CORS is blocked ([Gemini API key docs](https://ai.google.dev/gemini-api/docs/api-key)). Verify with one real key before building more.

The recommended order is below. Model names churn monthly, so each entry lives in a config file with a capability record (strict schema support, thinking toggle, language tier).

| Tier | Provider and model | Role | Caveat |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | User's Gemini key: Flash for the script pass, Flash-Lite for shot cards and edits | Default, the only recommended Nepali writer | Unpublished, low free quotas. Free-tier data may be used by Google. |
| 2 | User's Groq key: gpt-oss-120b or qwen3.8-27b with strict JSON | Fast second choice | Free-plan numbers are only in the console. Prose is likely dry (inference). Nepali is untested. |
| 3 | OpenRouter OAuth, free models (Gemma 4, Nemotron) | Choice for users who will not paste a key | 20 requests per minute, 50 per day unless the account bought 10 credits ([OpenRouter limits](https://openrouter.ai/docs/api-reference/limits)). |
| 4 | On-device WebLLM Qwen3.5-2B (about 2.2 GB VRAM), 4B as opt-in | Offline and private, English-only | At 2 to 4B, Hindi and Nepali are unreliable. Speed is unmeasured and may be minutes. |
| 5 | Rules and template | Never fails, never invents scores | Already built in M3a. |

Gemini and Groq both offer schema-constrained JSON, but Groq's strict mode covers only gpt-oss and qwen3.8-27b, and Llama 3.3 70B gets loose JSON ([Gemini structured output](https://ai.google.dev/gemini-api/docs/structured-output), [Groq structured outputs](https://console.groq.com/docs/structured-outputs)). Gemini documents that outputs can be valid JSON yet semantically wrong, so keep Zod validation and one repair retry. Reasoning models add hidden thinking tokens, so set low effort or turn thinking off.

When the user has no Gemini key, Introcut should say so plainly: Nepali is "experimental" on other providers, Hindi is fine, English is fine. It should also offer to write English and let the user translate. The research found no evidence that write-in-English-then-translate beats direct generation, and no clean permissive, browser-runnable Nepali translator exists. NLLB-200 is CC-BY-NC, and TranslateGemma lists 55 languages without Nepali and carries the custom Gemma terms. Never use Hindi as a pivot into Nepali, because it adds exactly the Hindi-register leakage that is the main expected failure ([TranslateGemma card](https://huggingface.co/google/translategemma-4b-it), [NLLB card](https://huggingface.co/facebook/nllb-200-distilled-600M/blob/main/README.md)).

## Why RAG loses here, and what to build instead

Retrieval adds a model download (23 MB for the int8 MiniLM, which is Apache-2.0), a worker, and a failure mode. It buys a gain nobody has measured on scripts under 100 words ([Xenova/all-MiniLM-L6-v2](https://huggingface.co/Xenova/all-MiniLM-L6-v2)). A library of 50 to 300 cards at 80 to 150 tokens each fits in a prompt, and selecting 5 of them costs under 800 tokens. Nearest-neighbour retrieval also returns the most typical pattern, which worsens the real problem. LLM-written stories are measurably more alike than human ones, and post-training reduces diversity ([arXiv 2606.17350](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2606.17350), [Peeperkorn thesis](https://kar.kent.ac.uk/116708/1/peeperkorn2026phdfinal.pdf)). The better fix is deliberate variety: pick one hook pattern at random, weighted by tags, on every run. Search delay is not the bottleneck either way. The latency that matters is the first model download and the LLM call itself, and both can be hidden by preloading while the user types.

The selection works like this. Code classifies the idea by keyword rules plus the user's chosen type. It picks one beat-sheet card for the type, one best-fit hook card, one weighted-random hook card, and an always-on do/don't card. A card is a short JSON record with a type, tags, the beat table, a hook pattern with a good and a bad example, and the checks it implies. Embedding search becomes worth adding only if the library passes about 500 cards or tag matching measurably fails, and then it should be BGE-small (MIT) or MiniLM, not EmbeddingGemma, whose Gemma license is not MIT/Apache style ([BGE-small](https://huggingface.co/BAAI/bge-small-en-v1.5), [EmbeddingGemma](https://huggingface.co/google/embeddinggemma-300m)). Brute-force cosine over 5,000 vectors takes a few milliseconds, so no vector database is needed (the researcher's arithmetic).

The legal data picture is simple. YouTube's terms bar accessing the service "using any automated means" without written permission ([YouTube Terms](https://www.youtube.com/t/terms)), and the Pile's scraped YouTube-subtitle subset drew press coverage as a terms problem ([The Register](https://www.theregister.com/2024/07/17/youtube_video_subtitles_ai/)). Whether analysing transcripts is fair use is unresolved, so do not scrape, and never ship copyrighted transcripts. The sound method is for the owner to watch references and write original structure notes. Ideas and structures are generally not protected, though that is general copyright principle, not sourced here, and a lawyer should confirm it if it matters. The data tiers, from most to least valuable and safe, are the owner's 15 to 30 pattern cards, 10 to 30 owner-written exemplar scripts under MIT or CC0 with beat annotations (these double as the eval set), public-domain folktales as plot seeds to be rewritten, not copied (check each translation's own copyright), and later opt-in user-contributed scripts under CC0. WritingPrompts (an MIT label on Reddit-sourced stories), TinyStories (CDLA-Sharing, a children's vocabulary) and Wikipedia plot text (CC-BY-SA) should stay offline, if used at all ([WritingPrompts](https://huggingface.co/datasets/euclaise/writingprompts), [TinyStories](https://huggingface.co/datasets/roneneldan/TinyStories), [Gutenberg](https://www.gutenberg.org/policy/permission.html)). Hand-edited cards are the safest position on both copyright and quality. Synthetic cards from a strong model are possible, but Gemini's terms bar building competing models and are silent on synthetic data, so label them and keep a human edit on each (verify each provider's terms).

For Hindi and Nepali, the cultural and register layer belongs in the cards too. Specificity should be a rule: a real district, crop and object (doko, sickle, bullock), a real stressor (monsoon timing, middleman price, a son abroad), and one "I know that feeling" detail per script. These are hypotheses to test with native readers, because audience research is thin. The one commercial analytics page found estimates a Hindi storytelling channel's audience as mostly rural and semi-urban families ([OutlierKit](https://outlierkit.com/channel/naitikkahaniya-hindi)), and that is one tool's estimate.

## A small pipeline, with the video prompts built by code

The default flow uses one to two LLM calls for the script and one separate shallow call for the shots. Call 1 sets temperature around 0.8 and returns JSON with the verdict or angle, logline, 5 to 6 timed beats, voice-over and a shot list. A validator then runs in code with no LLM. A second call fires only if the validator or a cheap self-score fails. For Hindi and Nepali that second call is a "native editor" pass that fixes Hindi words, honorific level and spoken style while keeping timing. Hierarchical outline-then-write has the strongest evidence (DOC beat its baseline by 22.5 points on plot coherence and 20.7 on interestingness; Re3 judged 14 points more coherent) ([DOC](https://arxiv.org/html/2212.10077v3), [Re3](https://arxiv.org/abs/2210.06774)). Those papers tested 3,500-word stories, so for 80 words a single structured call with explicit beats probably captures most of it. Self-Refine reports about 20% average gains but was tested on GPT-3.5 and GPT-4 and not broken out for creative writing ([Self-Refine](https://proceedings.neurips.cc/paper_files/paper/2023/hash/91edff07232fb1b55a505a9e9f6c0ff3-Abstract.html)). That is why the critique pass is conditional. The budget is about 4,000 tokens for a three-call run (the researcher's estimate). At 20 requests a day on Gemini Flash that is a handful of scripts a day, so cache by a hash of idea, type, settings and prompt version, and reuse the plan when only regenerating the script. Show quota from response headers where available, and back off on 429.

The validator is where most of the gain is, because code checks what LLMs get wrong. The checks are: word count within 8% of target, hook at most 9 words and not a filler opener ("Did you know", "Have you ever wondered"), average sentence length of 6 to 12 words and none over 18, shot durations that sum to the total, a stakes line by second 8, and the last line at most 14 words with no call to action. For Nepali there are cheap browser checks that need no model. The Devanagari ratio should be at least 0.97, with stray Latin letters, ASCII digits and Arabic-script characters flagged. A blocklist of Hindi-only function words (hai, nahi, kya, aur, bahut) catches the likeliest leak with high precision and modest recall. A Unicode normalisation check completes the set. GlotLID (Apache-2.0) could separate Nepali from Hindi per sentence, but its Nepali accuracy and a browser build are unconfirmed ([GlotLID](https://huggingface.co/cis-lmu/glotlid)).

The generator prompts should not be written freehand by the model. The model fills a strict JSON "shot card" for each clip (intent, generator, duration, one subject by ID, one action, setting, one camera move, audio, an avoid list as nouns, a risk level and a fallback shot), and code compiles it into each generator's text. That guarantees verbatim character and style bibles, separate audio sentences and colon-introduced dialogue, which Google's guide and community tips recommend ([Google Veo prompt guide](https://docs.cloud.google.com/vertex-ai/generative-ai/docs/video/video-gen-prompt-guide), [Replicate](https://replicate.com/blog/using-and-prompting-veo-3)). A lint then rejects output with no camera, text or counts in the scene, hand-work with no fallback, brands, minors, real people, dialogue over 20 words in a language the profile does not list, and totals outside 28 to 34 seconds. After one re-ask with the error list, the lint falls back to a template. The shot-design rules come from the weak points reported for current generators: hands, readable text, multi-person scenes, fast motion and long actions. So the writer should prefer one subject, one action and one move per 4 to 8 second clip, cutaways that show the result of a hand action, and the user's own footage for anything factual, a screen or a face ([Opus Pro](https://www.opus.pro/blog/ai-slop-aesthetic-12-tells), [Morphic](https://morphic.com/resources/how-to/ai-video-troubleshooting)).

Free-generator facts should ship as a small dated data file, because they conflict across sources and change often. Google's own help page says free Flow users get **50 credits per day**, with Veo 3.1 Lite at 10 credits for a 4, 6 or 8 second clip, and about five attempts a day ([Google Flow Help](https://support.google.com/flow/answer/16526234?hl=en)). That is one take of each shot of a five-clip story and no retries, so a story takes two to four days. Hailuo, Kling, Dreamina and PixVerse are lower-confidence and the dispute over their numbers is unresolved, as is Nepali speech in Veo. The shot-card dialogue field must therefore be null for any language the generator profile does not list as verified, with the line moved to voice-over. The default Nepali design is a closed-mouth or reaction clip plus an Introcut voice-over and captions. The same help page says free users may be locked out of video generation at peak hours (roughly 2 to 5 PM UTC, about 7:45 to 10:45 PM Nepal time), so the credit plan should say so. The 9:16 behaviour on free text-to-video, whether free output carries a visible watermark, and commercial rights on free outputs are all unverified.

## Judging quality without fooling yourself

Evaluation runs offline in the repo, not in the user's browser, and has four layers. The first layer is the deterministic checks above, run on every output at no cost. The second layer is a narrow-criteria rubric of eight 1-to-5 items with anchored descriptions: hook strength in the first 2 seconds, clarity (one idea, one protagonist), pacing against the word budget, specificity, payoff, relatability, spoken naturalness, and originality against recent outputs. EQ-Bench's experience supports narrow criteria over a single 0 to 10 score, and its instruction not to reward length ([EQ-Bench](https://sam-paech-eq-bench-leaderboard.static.hf.space/about.html)). Judges show verbosity and self-preference biases ([arXiv 2506.22316](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.22316v3)). So the third layer is a pairwise judge with positions swapped, a different model family from the generator, and length checked in code. The fourth layer is the owner, who is the only reliable judge of Nepali. There is no evidence on LLM-judge reliability for Nepali, so weight the judge below the owner.

The test set is 30 ideas across story types, including 10 for the Nepali protocol. The owner rates blind, in randomised order, about 45 seconds each, on five 1-to-5 items: sounds spoken and not translated, correct Nepali, honorific and tone fit, meaning matches the idea, and "would post as is". The owner also ticks failure tags and writes down the offending words, which feed the blocklist and the prompt's negative list. The matrix compares Gemini direct, Gemini English-then-translate, Gemini direct plus editor pass, and one open model on Groq. The decision rule is to adopt the setup with the highest "would post" and near-zero Hindi-word tags, and require 70% of scripts at 4 or above on the first two items. Owner-approved lines become licensed few-shot examples and the regression set. Run the same set after every prompt change. Compare four variants before adding any complexity: prompt only, prompt plus cards, plus the critique pass, and best-of-3. Keep the cheapest variant whose judged mean is within noise of the best. This is the only way to settle the order of prompt, cards and critique for 30-second scripts, since no direct benchmark exists. The 120 calls a 40-prompt run needs fit in one free-tier day only if the quota is at the generous end, and may need two or three days at 20 requests a day (the researcher's estimate).

## What to build next: M3b

The repo already has the `LlmProvider` interface, an OpenAI-compatible provider, the rules brain, the LLM brain with Zod validation and one repair retry, six formats with beats, and locks. So M3b should add quality on top, not replace anything. A reasonable order, in one working session each, is below. Everything is a recommendation, and all latency and call-count figures are estimates to measure.

| Step | What to build | Done when |
|---|---|---|
| 0 | One real-key smoke test: Gemini native `generateContent` from the browser, then Groq. Confirm CORS and a schema response. Also test one Gemini key from a non-EEA account. | A script comes back in the browser from a real key, or the Gemini CORS block is confirmed and Groq becomes the default. |
| 1 | The card schema in `src/ai/cards/` and the owner's first 15 cards: 5 to 6 story types, 2 to 3 hook patterns each, one do/don't card. Each card cites no creator and is owner-authored (MIT or CC0). | Cards load and pass a Zod check. |
| 2 | The rules selector (type, best-fit hook, random hook, do/don't) and the prompt assembly that injects the cards, the language and the word budget into call 1. | Same idea gives varied hooks across runs. |
| 3 | The validator with the deterministic checks and the Devanagari and Hindi-leak checks. Hindi and Nepali word budgets are warnings until TTS is calibrated. | Unit tests cover each check. |
| 4 | The conditional edit call, with the native-editor variant for Hindi and Nepali, locked fields respected as CLAUDE.md requires. | A failing script is repaired without touching a locked scene. |
| 5 | The eval harness: `eval/prompts.json`, a runner, a pairwise judge, the owner rating sheet for the 10 Nepali ideas. Record one baseline. | Baseline scores and the first 40 owner ratings are stored. |
| 6 | Hand over to M4: shot cards, the per-generator compiler, the lint and the generator profile file with a "last verified" date. | Each shot compiles to a Veo prompt that passes lint. |

Add the cache and quota display once the baseline exists. Each card, example, model or font goes into `CREDITS.md` with its licence in the same change, and any embedding model, if ever added, with its licence too.

## Conclusion

The research changes the question. The hard part of a 30-second script is not finding information, which is what RAG solves. It is making a small model commit to one premise, escalate it and stop, and then checking the result in code. That is why the owner's hand-written cards, the validator and the eval set are worth more than any index. The same logic argues for starting with measurement. Without even a 30-prompt eval set, extra calls, extra passes and a larger model would be guesses.

The honest limits remain large. Nepali quality has no generation benchmark, free quotas are unpublished and falling, the retention numbers are marketing folklore, and speaking rates for Hindi and Nepali are unmeasured. So Introcut should ship each of these as an editable setting, a labelled estimate or a gate, tell users when Nepali is experimental on their provider, and never promise virality, lip-sync or commercial rights on free generator output. The owner's own ratings will be the strongest evidence this project produces.
